Your body clock works best when your days start and end at roughly the same times. It uses those cues, morning light most of all, to time when you feel alert and when sleepiness arrives. When your sleep moves around a lot from one day to the next, the clock keeps having to catch up, a little like living with mild jet lag.

## Regular, not rigid

Regularity is about the overall pattern, not about hitting the same minute every day. A late night out or an early flight now and then hardly matters. What tends to show is a habit of swinging back and forth: late nights and long lie-ins on some days, early alarms on others. Studies that follow people's sleep timing find that those who sleep more regularly tend to feel more alert during the day, even when their total sleep is about the same.

## Social jetlag

The most common swing is the weekend shift. If you wake at 6:30 on workdays and at 9:30 on free days, part of your week runs in a different time zone, and Monday morning feels like an early flight home. Researchers call this social jetlag. They measure it as the gap between the middle of your sleep on free days and the middle of your sleep on workdays.

A few things keep the gap small:

- Wake within about an hour of your usual time on free days.
- Get daylight soon after you wake, especially after a late night.
- If you're short on sleep, a short early-afternoon nap tends to shift your rhythm less than a long lie-in.

## In Sleep Saver

**Keel** is a regularity score from 0 to 100, based on your last two weeks. It compares each moment of one day with the same moment a day later: asleep both times or awake both times counts as steady. A score of 80 or more reads Even keel, 70 to 79 Fair, and below 70 Choppy. It needs seven days of data before it shows a number.

Your Week plan shows a **Planned keel** too, worked out from the times you've set, and a short note when your free days drift far from your alarm days.
